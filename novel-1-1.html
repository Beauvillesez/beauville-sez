Beauville: Built by Migrants — 1.1

The Third Option

This is a work of speculative fiction. All characters, including public figures depicted herein, are used fictitiously.

April 2026. Vancouver.

The city was in the briefest of its beautiful seasons. Outside the convention centre, the cherry trees along the seawall were in full bloom. Pink-white petals drifted down in the Pacific wind, settling on the shoulders of security officers in dark suits, on streets closed off for the occasion, and onto the grey-green water of the bay, where they vanished without a sound.

The G7 summit was in its second day.

The agenda held the usual anxieties of the age: trade barriers, climate pledges, AI regulation, China policy. The leaders shook hands for the cameras, argued in the meeting rooms, and traded what they really thought in low voices in the lounges.

Migration was the last item of the afternoon, and no one expected anything of it. The subject had been discussed for thirty years. Every time, it began with “We attach great importance to this issue” and ended with each country going its own way.

The President of the European Union offered a single sentence of introduction: “This time we have a different proposal. Allow me to ask my aide, Marcus Lim, to give you a brief presentation.”

The room filled with the polite, weary clicks of interpretation headsets being tuned. Several leaders opened the project summary that had just been placed in front of them. The first page introduced the speaker:

Marcus Lim

Senior Aide to the President of the European Union

Born in Singapore

BA in Political Science, National University of Singapore

MSc in Public Policy and Governance, London School of Economics

Longstanding research on transnational migration and urban governance

Most of them glanced at it and turned the page. They had seen too many experts with impressive résumés.

Marcus stood, walked to the screen and opened his laptop. The first slide held only three words:

THE THIRD OPTION

“For thirty years, we have been paying for the same problem,” he said. “Either we use the welfare system to house large numbers of undocumented migrants, or we do everything we can to keep them out and send them back. Both approaches cost enormous sums. Neither has solved anything.”

He clicked to the second slide.

“In 2024, the German federal government spent €28 billion on refugees and migration.” He paused for a second. “And that is the figure after it fell from the year before.”

He moved to the next slide without looking toward the middle of the table.

“The bill the United States passed last year allocates more than $170 billion over four years to border and immigration enforcement. ICE detention alone will cost no less than $14 billion a year — about 60 percent more than the entire federal prison system.”

Someone in the room coughed softly.

The American President, seated in the middle, kept his eyelids lowered, his eyes half closed. No one could tell whether he was napping or thinking.

“And that doesn’t count what the other EU member states, Canada and Australia spend,” Marcus said. “What we spend on this problem every year would be enough to build another Singapore.”

Only one line remained on the screen:

Far less spent. Far more achieved.

“Why do I say far more achieved? Because we have always treated these people as a burden,” Marcus said. “But think about it. A person who leaves behind everything familiar, crosses deserts and oceans to reach a strange country, and stakes his life on a better one — that alone tells you what kind of person he is.”

He moved to the next slide. On the left were three large Chinese characters, 过江龙. On the right, a few lines of explanation in small English type.

Someone in the room tilted their head slightly.

“There’s an expression in Chinese,” Marcus said. He didn’t attempt the pronunciation. “It means a dragon that crosses the river. It describes people who leave their old territory, cross the water, and carve out a place for themselves somewhere new. The phrase carries a little wariness and a little respect — because a dragon from across the river usually works harder than the locals. It has no way back.”

What he didn’t say was that his great-grandfather had been one of those dragons. A hundred years ago, that young man from Fujian had carried sacks on the docks of Singapore for more than ten years, and died without ever going back to see the hills of home. As a boy, Marcus had seen only one photograph of him: a slight man standing in the doorway of a provision shop, squinting, as if the Nanyang sun were too bright for him to open his eyes.

“America and Canada were built by migrants. Australia and New Zealand were built by migrants. Singapore was built by migrants,” he said. “Everything we have today was left to us by some generation of people who had been turned away somewhere else.”

“So I want to propose a third option. Not just detention and deportation any more, and not just relief. We give them a place and a set of rules, and then we see what they can build — an entirely new special economic zone.”

Before he had finished, a voice came from the other end of the table. It was the British Prime Minister.

“What place?” She made no effort to hide the doubt in her voice. “That sounds lovely, sir. But there isn’t a single piece of unclaimed land on earth waiting for us to run an experiment on it.”

The French and Italian leaders nodded in agreement. The German, Canadian and Japanese leaders smiled and said nothing. The American President’s eyes stayed closed.

Marcus didn’t hurry to answer. He brought up a map of the South Pacific and zoomed in on the waters east of Australia and New Zealand. Out of the vast blue rose dots of every size, and many unfamiliar names.

“There is,” he said. “East of Australia and New Zealand there are a great many remote islands that have sat unused and undeveloped for a long time. Some are under Australian or New Zealand jurisdiction; many belong to small South Pacific nations. We find one and ask the sovereign state for a long-term lease. Sovereignty stays with the host country; the zone has administrative autonomy. It is far from the continents of Europe, Asia and the Americas. The migrants can leave the chaos they came from — and that chaos will find it hard to follow them.”

“Contributing countries pay according to the number of residents they send, and companies from those countries bid for the construction. Port, power grid, schools, laws — all built from zero.”

“This is not a camp. It is a special economic zone, a place for people willing to start again. In ten years it will be the West’s most important pool of migrant talent, and an emerging market in the Pacific that no one can ignore.”

The Australian Prime Minister, attending as an invited guest, raised an eyebrow. “Interesting. Go ahead.”

Marcus turned to him. “For Australia and New Zealand, it means a talent reserve and a new market on your doorstep. Most South Pacific island nations live on tourism, resource exports and foreign aid. The region has always lacked a growth engine that could lift all of it.”

The Australian Prime Minister didn’t reply. He only wrote a few words on the summary in front of him.

The Japanese Prime Minister looked thoughtful. “There are plenty of islands along the coasts of the Americas as well. They’re closer, and perhaps easier to manage. Have you not considered them?”

A brief silence fell over the room.

For the first time, the American President opened his eyes all the way.

“No way,” he said, with unusual decisiveness. “Too close to America. They’d just get on boats and come in again. We don’t want them.”

Someone pressed their lips together without expression. Someone gave him a sideways look. No one responded.

“Finding the right island, and a host country willing to work with us for the long term, is the first step,” Marcus said calmly. “That work can begin as soon as today is over.”

The American President chimed in again. “If Australia were willing, we could settle it right now.”

The Australian Prime Minister slowly turned his head to look at him and gave a short laugh. “Mr. President, that’s not a decision I get to make on my own.”

The whole table burst out laughing.

The Italian Prime Minister turned to the room, clearly enjoying himself. “Before we find this place, why don’t we give it a name?”

“Good idea.” The French President rested his left elbow on the arm of his chair, chin in hand, as if thinking.

The American President seemed uninterested. “Call it whatever you like. Honestly, my name would suit it best — but I know you probably won’t agree.” With that, he closed his eyes and seemed to fall asleep again.

The others exchanged glances. No one said anything.

The French President took his hand from his chin and leaned forward. “What about Beaupays? Beautiful land.”

The Canadian Prime Minister asked quietly, “Beautiful country?”

The Australian Prime Minister said at once, “No. We don’t want a new country next door.”

A few faces around the table struggled to hold back smiles, but no one laughed out loud.

The corner of the Canadian Prime Minister’s mouth lifted. “What about Beauville? Beautiful town.”

“I like that,” the British Prime Minister said. “Plato called his ideal city Kallipolis — in Greek, the beautiful city. If we call this new migrant community Beauville, perhaps it can become a modern Kallipolis.”

The other leaders nodded. The President of the European Union looked at Marcus. “Marcus, make a note. From now on, this plan is called Project Beauville.”

Marcus typed a few keystrokes, and a cover-style slide appeared on the screen:

PROJECT BEAUVILLE

“A very beautiful name,” he said. “I always learn something new in your company.”

Around the table, people were still laughing quietly. The American President’s breathing had grown slow and even.

Marcus looked at the two words on the screen and thought of his great-grandfather’s photograph. That slight man squinting in the doorway of a provision shop could never have imagined that a hundred years later, a group of the most powerful people in the world would sit together and name a city that did not yet exist.

Outside the window, the cherry blossoms were still falling, one petal after another, onto the grey-green water of the bay.
