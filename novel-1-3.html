Beauville: Built by Migrants — 1.3

The Stages, and the Man Who Stopped Listening

This is a work of speculative fiction. All characters, including public figures depicted herein, are used fictitiously.

Marcus switched to the next slide. The abstract idea had become a clear timeline: A Three-Stage Development Path — From Capital Launch to Full Export-Driven Independence.

“If governance is the skeleton, the economy is the blood.”

He pointed to the first dark green block: Stage 01, Capital Launch and Infrastructure, Years 1 to 3.

“The first three years lay the foundation. I’ve already said where the money comes from, so I’ll make just one point: the people on the island are not waiting for handouts. They are drawing wages. After basic training, migrants go straight to work. They build the port, the grid, their own homes, and they trade their wages for shares in future property. From the first day, they are small shareholders in this land, not parasites.”

The French President underlined something in the document in front of him.

Marcus’s hand moved to the teal block in the middle: Stage 02, Micro-Economic Autonomy, Years 4 to 7.

“Once the infrastructure is built, wages begin to circulate on the island. Some people open shops, some run logistics, some build houses. Corporate and personal income taxes are close to zero, and there are almost no barriers to starting a business. At this stage, residents go from builders to operators, and the transfusions from outside drop sharply.”

Finally his hand stopped on the gold lettering: Stage 03, Export-Driven Opening, Year 8 Onward. His voice rose slightly.

“This is the moment you’ve all been waiting for. From year eight, a modern port and a highly motivated workforce will draw in manufacturing, digital services, tech outsourcing and maritime logistics, one after another. Export revenue will fully replace external subsidies. The zone will no longer be a burden but a net contributor to the region — and your most stable trading partner in the South Pacific.”

“Eight years.” Marcus turned and met the American President’s eyes, which were now open. “In just eight years, we can turn a border problem that burns money every minute into a blue-ocean asset that turns a profit every year. Instead of paying others to take in refugees, we will have invested in a future gateway to the Pacific.”

The American President laid both hands flat on the table and leaned forward slightly. This time, he was genuinely interested.

“I don’t care much about what happens in eight years. What I want to know is — can this thing start sooner? So we can get all the people we’re holding shipped out sooner?”

Someone in the room shifted their chair.

Marcus didn’t pause. “Yes. Once the site is confirmed, the first residents could be moved within six months. The island will need a workforce for the early infrastructure anyway.”

The American President nodded, leaned toward one side of his chair, and his expression eased a little.

“Good. Now, the construction side—” he paused, as if choosing his words, though he wasn’t choosing anything, “my son-in-law Gary has a lot of experience there. His company has done plenty of big real estate projects. He’d handle the infrastructure better than anyone. You should give him the big projects. That way, you might get your funding from me sooner.”

No one responded right away.

The Italian Prime Minister twisted toward the door, as if he would have liked nothing better than to get up and leave.

The British Prime Minister turned her head and gave the President a meaningful look.

The Japanese Prime Minister covered her mouth with her hand; there was a glint of laughter in her eyes she couldn’t quite hide.

The Canadian Prime Minister looked at the ceiling.

The President of the European Union picked up his glass, took a sip of water, and said calmly, “All construction projects will go through open international tender. Companies from every contributing country may bid, and contracts will be awarded on qualifications and price.”

“Yes,” the French President added. “That’s how projects work.”

The EU President paused, then added, “Of course, any qualified American company is welcome to bid — including your son-in-law’s.”

Marcus stood quietly, pressed his lips together, blinked once, and looked down to advance to the next slide.

At that moment, the door opened a crack. An agent stepped in, light on his feet, and bent to whisper a few words in the American President’s ear.

The President’s face changed abruptly. He drew his hands back from the table, stood up and straightened his suit.

“Excuse me,” he said, far more subdued than before. “I have something more urgent to take care of. Carry on, and send the documents to my office when you’re done.”

He turned and walked toward the door, half a beat faster than when he had come in.

The others, somewhat taken aback, said their goodbyes and watched him go.

The door closed.

A moment of silence.

Several people glanced at their phones at almost the same time.

Urgent briefings from their embassies and consulates in the United States, all saying much the same thing: immigration protests in several American cities had spun out of control and turned into armed clashes, with people on opposing sides attacking one another. The National Guard had been deployed, several city blocks were on fire, and casualty figures were not yet known.

No one read it aloud.

The French President turned his phone face down on the table.

The German Chancellor put down his phone, took off his glasses, looked at them, and put them back on.

The Japanese Prime Minister stared at her screen, one hand over her mouth again. This time there was no laughter in her eyes.

The EU President looked at the empty chair, then turned to Marcus, his voice calm. “Marcus, let’s continue.”

Marcus was about to move to the next slide when the French President raised his hand slightly, as if he had something to say.
