Beauville: Built by Migrants — 1.4

History Has Already Rendered Its Verdict

This is a work of speculative fiction. All characters, including public figures depicted herein, are used fictitiously.

The French President lowered his raised hand. “Marcus, would you mind going back one slide?”

“Of course.” Marcus returned to the Three-Stage Development Path.

The French President shifted in his seat and folded his arms across his chest. His tone was even. “This eight-year plan sounds very attractive. But if it isn’t self-sufficient by year eight, who fills the hole?”

“That is exactly the question we had to answer for ourselves first, Mr. President.” Marcus clicked to the next slide. “The reason we dared to write ‘independence in eight years’ into the timeline is that the logic underneath this project isn’t charity. It’s hedging. In case the internal economy doesn’t take off, we have built three firewalls. I’ll speak only to the first.”

“The money invested in the first three years won’t turn into a bubble. It will turn into a deep-water port, a container terminal, a power plant and an industrial park. These are hard assets. If the zone runs a fiscal deficit in year eight, a debt-for-equity swap is triggered automatically, and the consortium of contributing countries takes over the operating rights and revenues of these facilities.”

“The port.” The French President did not unfold his arms. “And the people who live around the port?”

Marcus glanced at the screen. There was nothing on that slide about them.

“They remain residents of the zone.”

“Residents of a zone owned by its creditors.”

Somewhere down the table, a pen stopped moving.

Marcus let that pass. “The other two firewalls — political risk guarantees from the multilateral development banks, and a reverse adjustment of talent quotas — are set out in full in the materials in front of you. In the worst case, the losses each country bears would be a very small figure relative to its national budget.”

A low murmur went around the room.

Marcus moved to the next slide. The title was stark and forceful:

History Has Already Rendered Its Verdict

“To see the difference between ‘pure welfare aid’ and ‘infrastructure-driven development,’ we don’t need to project the future. We only need to look back at history.” He walked to the screen and pointed to the green block on the left: Hainan Island.

“When it became a special economic zone in 1988, it was widely regarded as a backwater — barren, closed off, a place where convicts had been exiled in ancient times. Yet within a single generation, it became a thriving region open to the world. The secret was simple: concentrate limited capital in infrastructure, and attract hundreds of thousands of pioneers who wanted to change their lives. Geographic isolation gave room for trial and error, clear rules lowered costs, and economic freedom released human initiative.”

His gaze moved to the deep reddish-brown block on the right, and his voice cooled.

“On the other side is Gaza. For decades, it has been one of the largest recipients of foreign aid per capita anywhere in the world. But under prolonged blockade and pressure, that money never became a real working economy. When a place has no productive capacity of its own, transfusions from outside end up only as dependency passed from one generation to the next. And soil left barren long enough will, sooner or later, grow extremism.”

He looked straight down the length of the table.

“Our current migration policy is, in essence, a magnified version of that pure welfare model. What Beauville sets out to do is close off the old road and replicate the new one.”

The British Prime Minister had been leaning back in her chair, listening. Now she spoke.

“An island far from any continent. The money comes from foreigners, the rules are set by outsiders, and anyone who wants to leave has to wait seven years,” she said slowly. “With respect, Mr. Lim, are you sure it looks more like Hainan than like Gaza?”

The room went quiet for a moment.

“Madam Prime Minister, Beauville is by its nature more like Hainan,” Marcus said. “There is no historical baggage, no territorial conflict, no generations-old feud wearing people down. Everyone comes voluntarily, to start a new life, and the rules are written down clearly before they set foot on the island.”

“On a blank sheet of paper, what decides the outcome is governance.” He paused. “And one more thing: the people of Gaza can’t get out. Here there is a door. Seven years of obeying the law, and you can walk through it.”

“Provided the door stays open,” the British Prime Minister said. “And the key is in the hands of the people at this table.”

Marcus paused for a second. “Which is why it needs the commitment of everyone here.”

The British Prime Minister said nothing more. She only smiled — a smile that made it clear she did not consider that an answer.

Marcus tapped the keyboard, and the screen switched to Social and Educational Design. Six modules spread out across a dark blue background.

“Factories and ports decide whether a community survives. But what it becomes is decided by the generation that grows up there.” He pointed to the red school icon in the upper left. “So I’ll talk only about the first item: the boarding school system.”

“No society can complete integration with the first generation of migrants alone. What truly decides the future is the next generation. So we must make sure that children from every background have equal access to a modern civic education.”

“Children in the zone board at school from Monday to Friday and go home at weekends. Teachers are recruited from all over the world. The children study science, art and sport, and learn to think for themselves, with teaching materials grounded in the principles of equality, freedom, objectivity and reason. Those who want to go to university can apply anywhere in the world. For those who don’t want an academic path, we give priority to vocational and technical schools, so that everyone can earn a living once they leave home.”

“And schools here have a condition no immigrant country has ever had: every child comes from a migrant background. There is no dominant local group to shut them out. That shared footing is itself the most powerful force against extremism. When everyone is a builder, the divide between ‘us’ and ‘them’ simply ceases to exist.”

“When these children grow up, they won’t be merely members of a tribe, a religion or an ethnic group. They will be people who understand different civilizations and move freely among them.”

The Italian Prime Minister leaned over and quietly asked the Canadian Prime Minister what he thought.

It was a moment before the Canadian Prime Minister answered, his voice so low that only the Italian Prime Minister heard: “We had boarding schools in my country too.”

The Italian Prime Minister looked at him and asked nothing more.

Marcus didn’t hear it. His finger had already moved on to the other modules: healthcare and the funding model, the details already in the materials; points-based advancement — “seven years,” he said, glancing at the Canadian Prime Minister, “which we’ve already discussed.” Last came the scales icon in the centre: Rule of Law and Zero Tolerance. He had covered this when answering the German Chancellor, so this time he said little, simply letting the screen rest on the slide for a few seconds.

The slide listed four lines:

1. Violent crime, gang activity and acts that subvert the order of the zone will be severely punished according to law.

2. Those convicted will be imprisoned, or may earn a reduced sentence through physical labour.

3. The most serious crimes will carry the most severe punishments.

4. These rules are made clear before entry. Entry constitutes acceptance.

At the other end of the table, the German Chancellor picked up his pen and circled the third line in the annex.

“These modules form a self-consistent social ecosystem,” Marcus said. “We filter out crime with iron rules, reshape the next generation through education, and give everyone a legal way forward through points.”

The French President had said nothing for some time. Now he unfolded his arms.

“Mr. Lim,” he said, “you’ve told us who pays.”

He paused.

“But you still haven’t told us who is responsible.”

Marcus’s hand stopped on the mouse. He was silent for two seconds.

“Mr. President, I’d like to save that question for the end,” he said. “Because the answer depends on the next few slides.”

The French President neither agreed nor objected. He only lifted his hand slightly, signalling him to go on.
