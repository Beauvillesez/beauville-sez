ABOUT BEAUVILLE SEZ

A civic experiment on an island, imagined as a future you can help design.

"
Beauville is not a utopia. It is a serious attempt to imagine, from the ground up, a place where those who live among us — but cannot land anywhere — might finally take root.

Beauville SEZ is a fictional yet rigorously articulated special economic zone set in the South Pacific. It treats speculative fiction as a tool for institutional design: a place where migration, governance, and cultural life are modeled with the seriousness of a constitutional project and the openness of a shared narrative.

The story follows a proposal born in a G7 summit room, negotiated into reality one deal at a time. What emerges is not a utopia and not a rescue — but a working attempt to ask what a fairer, more honest arrangement between nations, capital, and displaced people might actually look like.

THE PREMISE IN ONE LINE

A special economic zone governed under a Managed Autonomy Model — sponsor nations hold defined mandates, the host nation retains sovereignty, and residents build a life within rules they did not write.

BSEZ is not a blueprint for escape, nor a self-governed utopia. It is a disciplined exploration of how irregular migration — a challenge so many countries are struggling to resolve — might have a solution that redistributes risk and reward more honestly than the two approaches we've already tried.

01

The island setting

Beauville is imagined on a temperate South Pacific island where trade winds, volcanic slopes, and a working harbor create a natural stage for international exchange. The geography is not decoration; it is a governance constraint.

Scarcity of land, the dignity of ecosystems, and distance from established capitals shape a society that must plan carefully, build compactly, and remain accountable to place.

02

A proposal, not a movement

The story centers on people who did not choose this place so much as arrive at it — irregular migrants from various backgrounds, stuck between the country they fled and the one that wouldn't take them. Beauville was not built by them; it was designed for them, in a G7 summit room, by policymakers weighing fiscal cost against political risk.

Migration is not treated as a problem to be managed by charity or deportation, but as labor and potential to be organized — for better or worse. What the migrants make of the structure they're handed is the story.

03

A managed, not an autonomous, zone

Every society carries its contradictions — invisible hierarchies, unspoken exclusions, conflicts no policy fully resolves. Beauville does not escape this by giving people a blank slate to govern themselves. It escapes it, if at all, through an unusual division of power: security, education, public health, and capital each answer to a different sponsor nation; the host nation holds final sovereignty and veto.

What happens when people from entirely different backgrounds start over inside a system they didn't design — that is the question this project asks, not "what would people build if left alone."

04

The digital gateway: E-Residency

In Beauville's founding charter, sovereignty extends into the digital realm through its E-Residency program — a fictional jurisdiction imagined as a haven for global freelancers, indie developers, and digital creators.

Within the story's economic model, a digital passport lets remote builders establish ventures in minutes, taxed at a transparent 5% micro-rate designed to reward rather than extract.

05

An editorial project with public architecture

Every symbol, vote, and policy draft is part of a shared story still being written. Readers are invited to move from observer to participant — to propose, debate, and help shape what Beauville becomes.

The tone is literary, but the commitments are civic. The story gains credibility because it is built in public.

06

The people in this story are not far from you

These are the people around us. Families navigating the impossible. Those already gone, given in fiction the world they were owed in life. And those still waiting — with less than refugees, and nowhere to call a beginning.

You may know someone like this. You may be someone like this.

07

What to expect

  • A speculative novel and the world it builds — characters, governance, and founding documents.
  • An evolving hybrid space, where the boundary between a speculative novel and real-world civic experiments blurs.
  • An invitation to ask whether the world's hardest problems — sovereignty, migration, belonging — might have solutions we haven't yet tried.
08

Why the project exists

In 2025 alone, federal spending on migration enforcement and welfare ran into the hundreds of billions across G7 nations — money that, as one architect of the plan puts it, "built nothing and produced no return."

Beauville imagines a third option: redirecting that spending from detention and deportation into infrastructure, in exchange for a fixed number of years of labor and a clear, if narrow, path to legal status elsewhere. It is not a rescue. It is a trade — and the story does not pretend otherwise.
