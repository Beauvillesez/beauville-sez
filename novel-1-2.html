Beauville: Built by Migrants — 1.2

Two Approaches, Both Failing

This is a work of speculative fiction. All characters, including public figures depicted herein, are used fictitiously.

Marcus didn’t move to the next slide right away.

“Before I go on, I’d like to talk about a word,” he said. “A word many of us use almost every day.”

He looked around the table.

“Illegal immigrant.”

Several leaders looked up.

“Our press releases say it. Our parliaments say it. Our voters say it. Once a person is called ‘illegal,’ he is no longer a carpenter, a nurse, a father. He is nothing but a problem to be dealt with. And when it comes to dealing with problems, we only know two methods.”

“But change the word, change the way we look — and they are not a problem population. They are human capital. They are the raw material of every great act of building in human history.”

He clicked to the next slide.

Two Approaches, Both Failing

On the left of the screen, in dark blue: “The Welfare Dependency Trap.” On the right, a column of conspicuous yellow text: “The Forced Deportation Fallacy.”

“The first approach is compassion. We pay for their housing and their allowances, and they end up stuck in settlement estates on the edge of our cities, a parallel society cut off from the mainstream. Taxpayers grow angrier and the far right keeps rising.” He didn’t linger on the slide. “The second approach is deportation. Enforcement and removals cost tens of billions every year, produce nothing, and leave only headline images that embarrass the West.”

“We have walked both roads. Both are dead ends.”

From the middle of the table came the soft creak of a chair. The American President had opened his eyes.

“All this talk,” he said. “How much is this going to cost me?”

Some people looked down at their papers. Some looked out the window.

“No additional money, Mr. President,” Marcus said. “Every contributing country already has a budget for migrant settlement and enforcement — money that is already being spent. We convert part of it into interest-free loans for the zone’s start-up fund. Until now, that money bought deportation flights, detention beds and legal fees, and once it was spent, it was gone. Now the same money becomes a deep-water port, a power grid and schools. Those are assets. They generate returns.”

“We are not talking about charity. This is an angel investment.”

“Angel investment.” The American President repeated the words, as if weighing them. “I like investments.”

The French President leaned forward. “An investment means equity. Who holds the shares? And who is being invested in — the island, or the people?”

“In the early phase, infrastructure is exchanged for equity, and the construction firms take the future returns on those assets. After eight years, the zone pays its own way, and the contributing countries don’t put in another cent,” Marcus said. “As for who is being invested in—” he paused, “the people. The island is only a place.”

The French President asked nothing more and sat back in his chair.

The German Chancellor, silent until now, finally spoke, his voice low. “Forgive me for interrupting. Even if sovereignty and money are both resolved, there is a central question: who is responsible for law and order in this zone? If it becomes a vast lawless territory with an advanced port, we won’t have created a Singapore. We’ll have created a pirate haven — or worse, a slum the size of a sovereign state.”

Marcus didn’t smile.

“I grew up in Singapore, Chancellor,” he said. “When I was a boy, you couldn’t chew gum in the street, littering got you fined, and serious crimes got you caned. Many of my European friends think that is too harsh. But my great-grandfather’s generation survived on the docks and on the turf of the secret societies. They knew what life without order looks like.”

“So this is not an anarchist utopia. The zone follows the Singapore model of strict rule of law: those who obey the law are fully protected; those who break it are severely punished. Everyone is told these rules in black and white at the moment of entry, and only those who accept them may come. That in itself is a filter. The people who stay are the ones who truly want order, and who are willing to change their lives with their own hands.”

He brought up a multi-layered governance diagram.

“We call it the Managed Autonomy Model, jointly backed by everyone at this table. The full structure is in the annex in front of you.”

The German Chancellor turned to that page of the annex and studied it for a long time. He said nothing more.

Marcus moved to the next slide: Project Overview — An Offshore Autonomous Special Economic Zone. Four blue blocks stood in a row: Location, Governance, Economy, Talent.

“The details of the first three are in the documents in front of you. I’ll speak only to the last, because it is the most valuable to every country at this table: the talent pipeline.”

“The first generation settles and works here. Their children will grow up in a curriculum fully compatible with your own countries’ systems. We have set a firm benchmark: seven years of good standing. Anyone who has lived and worked here for seven years without a criminal record or serious violation can exchange that for a globally recognized work permit.”

“By then, they are no longer ‘border-jumpers’ or ‘refugees.’ They are a rigorously screened, productive, law-abiding workforce, moving legally to the contributing countries. Your factories, your tech outsourcing centres, your ageing populations will all receive the cleanest, safest possible supply.”

The Canadian Prime Minister had been absently turning the coffee cup in his hands. Now he set it down.

“I have a question,” he said. “Someone spends six years on the island, keeps his head down, and in the sixth year makes a mistake — gets into a fight, say, or steals something. What happens to him? Does the seven years start over, or is he simply out?”

Marcus’s hand stayed on the mouse.

“The rule is clear,” he said evenly. “Just as many professions require a criminal background check, a record means losing eligibility. As for what degree of misconduct can be tolerated—” he looked across the table, “that is exactly what we can decide together.”

The Canadian Prime Minister asked nothing more and picked his coffee cup back up.

At the other end of the table, the German Chancellor picked up his pen and wrote a single word in the margin of the annex.
