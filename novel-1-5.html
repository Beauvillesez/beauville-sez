Beauville: Built by Migrants — 1.5

Religion and Public Order

This is a work of speculative fiction. All characters, including public figures depicted herein, are used fictitiously.

Marcus moved to the next slide. Its title was only a few words: Religion and Public Order.

“This next point,” he said, his tone neither heavy nor light, “is also the one most easily avoided.”

“Beauville treats every religious faith equally, and residents enjoy full freedom of private belief. But the zone’s public space is neutral.”

He paused.

“To that end, there are three principles we must hold to.”

1. Religious activity is confined to private homes and registered places of worship. No religious ritual or religious promotion of any kind may take place in public spaces.

2. The total floor area of a basic place of worship is limited to 300 square metres, with no more than 200 seats. A special permit must be obtained from the Zone Administrative Committee before construction.

3. No registered place of worship may be financed from abroad in the name of religious donations.

“This is not discrimination against any religion. It is to ensure that no single faith can occupy public space.”

“Faith is, at heart, a private relationship between a person and the god he believes in. Its true value is that it gives a person peace and strength in his darkest hour. In private, that strength is pure and noble. But when it is used to mobilize and inflame a group, it can turn into social unrest and ethnic conflict. So limiting the size of places of worship is a way of using institutions to let faith do what it does best — to return it to the heart and keep it away from politics.”

He looked around the room.

“The people of Beauville will hold all kinds of beliefs. But their shared sense of belonging won’t be built on faith. It will be built on the experience of building this city together.”

As soon as he finished, the Australian Prime Minister nodded. “Makes sense.” His tone was calm, but it carried weight. “We’ve seen too many island governments torn apart by clan and faith. The last thing Canberra wants is another mess in our backyard where people point guns at each other under the banner of religion.”

“But this is rather too radical, and too idealistic.” The British Prime Minister shook her head, her expression turning serious. “We cannot restrict people’s freedom of belief on the first page of the zone’s charter.”

The German Chancellor rubbed his temples, his voice carrying the particular gravity of the European tradition. “The Prime Minister is right. Freedom of belief is indivisible. Using administrative power to carve up religion will only drive it underground and provoke a backlash. History shows that the more the space for faith is suppressed, the more aggressive faith tends to become.”

Marcus looked calmly at the two leaders.

“Madam Prime Minister, Chancellor — the migrants who go to Beauville will come from different countries, different faiths, different sects. If we don’t set the rules from the first day, they will soon start competing: whose house of worship is bigger, whose congregation is larger. Faith will drift away from its core.”

“We are limiting scale, not faith itself. They can still pray. They can still find peace in their faith. We simply won’t allow any group to capitalize on faith and turn it into a tool of mobilization.”

“Half of these rules we learned from Singapore. There, building a place of worship requires a permit, and foreign funding of religious groups is regulated. The size limit is our own addition, because we are starting from zero and have the chance to set the rules before the first brick is laid. Since its founding, Singapore has never again seen large-scale religious conflict.”

He saw the Japanese Prime Minister nodding, looked at the grave faces of the others, and said, word by word:

“We are investing enormous sums to build a prosperous economy — not a second Lebanon or Syria.”

The British Prime Minister and the German Chancellor exchanged a look. The British Prime Minister spoke: “We’ll need to come back to this one.” The German Chancellor said nothing, and turned the annex in front of him back to the page he had circled.

The Canadian Prime Minister looked thoughtful but said nothing. The Italian Prime Minister’s face was expressionless, as if he were still turning it over.

Marcus didn’t rush into the implementation details. Instead he brought up another slide, titled: The Institutional Paradox.

“Before we discuss technical details, we have to face a paradox we can’t avoid — one that may even make some of you uncomfortable.”

He pointed to the large text in the middle of the screen:

A zone originally created to resettle undocumented people — through strict rule of law, low taxes, a highly motivated population and top-tier educational outcomes — will ultimately surpass many traditional Western metropolises in economic vitality and social cohesion.

“Your countries have centuries of accumulated civilization, the finest universities, the deepest pools of capital. But you also carry heavy burdens: the fiscal inertia of generous welfare, declining judicial efficiency, the stagnation that comes with ageing, and ever deeper divisions between classes.”

“Beauville is a blank sheet of paper.” Marcus paused. “It has no tug-of-war of electoral politics, no tangled web of entrenched interests. Structural reforms that political resistance will never let you push through in your own countries can be running on this island from the first day.”

He pointed to the dark blue block at the bottom: Twenty Years On.

“What happens in twenty years? The zone’s graduates will have grown up with modern civic values. They will speak several languages and have entrepreneurial experience earned the hard way in a low-tax offshore market. More importantly, every one of them will have a clean seven-year record.”

The corners of Marcus’s mouth lifted slightly. He was himself descended from a dragon that had crossed the river.

“They are exactly the kind of people your countries will need most: skilled industrial workers, talent for the digital age. This talent pipeline is built in, pre-screened, and entirely self-financing. Your countries won’t need to spend a cent training them. They will have done it all themselves.”

The Australian Prime Minister, who had been listening all along, suddenly asked, “If the best people all leave, who’s going to build your Singapore?”

Marcus glanced at the Canadian Prime Minister. “Every year, top talent leaves Canada for the United States. But Canada hasn’t declined because of it, because more people come in every year. Beauville will be the same: when some walk out, new people walk in. And Beauville will also stay open to professionals in every field, from anywhere in the world, who want to come. People move. The city stays.”

The Canadian Prime Minister smiled and didn’t deny it.

At the very bottom of the slide, a line of italic text stood out against the dark background:

Singapore proved it: an island with no resources can become a global benchmark of prosperity through institutional discipline and openness to talent.

“This is not a refuge, ladies and gentlemen.” Marcus straightened. “It is an institutional incubator. If you keep these people behind your border walls, they will forever be a problem you can’t solve. But if you put them into this machine, in twenty years this zone, built by undocumented migrants, will become another Singapore.”

The room was very quiet.

No one objected. Whether because they had been persuaded, or because no one at this table was willing to admit that their own country could no longer push those reforms through, it was impossible to say.

The Italian Prime Minister, expressionless until now, stared for a long while at the words “surpass many traditional Western metropolises” on the screen, then said quietly:

“This investment sounds more and more like the real thing. So…” He glanced at the Australian Prime Minister beside him. “We said earlier that this zone would go on some island in the South Pacific. Has anyone thought about how you get a country to hand over an island like that?”
